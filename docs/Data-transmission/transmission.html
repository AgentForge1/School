🎯 Starter Activity – The Tin Can Telephone

A tin can telephone allows one person to transmit a message in binary by saying 1 or 0.

How is it possible for both people to send a message to each other?

How could messages be sent faster?

Discuss with your partner — be ready to share your ideas.

Show suggested answer
  • They would need to swap the cans from ear to mouth and vice versa.
  • A keyword such as 'over' could be used to signify the end of a transmission in one direction.
  • Two connections could be made – one for listening and one for speaking – in Computer Science this is known as half-duplex.
  • Alternatively, they could speak each digit faster – this is a serial transmission and the way that technologies such as USB are faster than older technologies such as computer serial ports.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe how data is transmitted using serial and parallel methods.
  • Explain the suitability, advantages and disadvantages of each method.
  • Describe simplex, half-duplex and full-duplex transmission.
  • Understand the USB interface and explain how it is used to transmit data.
Key Fact: The tin can telephone is a half-duplex system — both people can send messages, but only one at a time. Modern networks have evolved far beyond this, but the same fundamental ideas still apply.

Serial Transmission

Bits are sent one signal at a time over a single wire.

  • ✔ Only one wire needed — cheaper and smaller cables
  • ✔ Can be used over longer distances without crosstalk or skew
  • ✔ Modern processors are fast enough to handle high serial speeds
  • ✘ Historically slower than parallel (but no longer true)

Examples: USB, SATA, Ethernet, broadband internet

Parallel Transmission

Multiple wires are used so that many bits may be sent at the same time.

  • ✔ Faster than serial if the same data rate is used per wire
  • ✔ Still used inside computers (CPU ↔ RAM)
  • ✘ Suffers from crosstalk (signals interfering)
  • ✘ Suffers from skew (signals arriving at slightly different times)
  • ✘ Requires many more wires — thicker, more expensive cables

Examples: Older PATA/IDE hard drive cables, parallel printer cables

Key point: Parallel cables were common before the year 2000, but they have been replaced by SATA (Serial ATA) and USB (Universal Serial Bus) cables. Why? Because serial transmission avoids the problems of crosstalk and skew, and as processors became faster, serial transmission became the faster option.

🛑 Stop & Check 2

Class check: Your teacher will spin the wheel to pick students to share one reason why serial transmission replaced parallel transmission for external cables.

Suggested answer:
Parallel cables suffer from crosstalk and skew, which get worse over longer distances. Before the year 2000, processors were not fast enough to keep up with very fast serial transmission, so parallel was used to increase overall speed. As processors became faster, serial transmission could achieve higher speeds without crosstalk or skew. Serial cables also require far fewer wires, making them cheaper to make and smaller, and they allow for longer cables without the risks of crosstalk and skew.

Simplex

Data travels in one direction only.

  • ✔ Simple and cheap
  • ✔ Entire bandwidth used for one direction
  • ✘ Cannot send and receive

Example: Keyboard → computer

Half-Duplex

Data can travel in both directions along a single connection, but not at the same time.

  • ✔ Both devices can send and receive
  • ✘ Cannot send and receive simultaneously

Example: Walkie talkies

Full-Duplex

Data can travel in both directions simultaneously using two cables (or two fibres).

  • ✔ Fastest — can send and receive at the same time
  • ✔ Full bandwidth in both directions
  • ✘ Requires more cables/infrastructure

Example: Ethernet network cables, fibre optic cables

Key Insight

These terms can be combined with serial and parallel:

  • A serial, simplex cable sends one bit at a time in one direction only.
  • A parallel, full-duplex cable sends multiple bits at a time in both directions simultaneously.

🛑 Stop & Check 3

Class check: Your teacher will spin the wheel and ask you to explain the difference between half-duplex and full-duplex using a real-world analogy.

Suggested answer:
Half-duplex is like a single-lane road with traffic lights — cars can travel in both directions, but only one direction at a time. Full-duplex is like a dual carriageway or motorway — cars can travel in both directions simultaneously. (The tin can telephone is half-duplex; a modern phone call is full-duplex.)

Universal Serial Bus (USB) is a serial cable commonly used to connect external devices to computers, laptops, tablets and smartphones. It can be plugged in or removed whilst the device is turned on (hot-swappable). Older serial and parallel cables required the computer to be turned off first.

USB Benefits

  • ✔ Hot-swappable — plug in/remove whilst powered on
  • ✔ USB 1: 1.5–12 Mbps; USB 3: up to 20 Gbps
  • ✔ No screws needed — simple connector
  • ✔ All sorts of devices can be connected by installing the necessary drivers

USB Drawbacks

  • ✘ Connections should only be 5 metres in length before a USB hub is used
  • ✘ Not fast enough for internal devices such as CPU and hard drive (DDR4 RAM can reach 200 Gbps — about 10× faster than USB 3)

Plenary — Exit Ticket (5 mins)

Let's review what we learned today.

1. What is the difference between serial and parallel transmission?

Serial: bits are sent one signal at a time over a single wire. Parallel: multiple wires are used so that many bits may be sent at the same time.

2. What are the two main problems with parallel transmission over long distances?

Crosstalk — a signal on one line creates a disturbance in another line, potentially causing a bit to be reversed. Skew — each wire has slightly different properties, so signals arrive at slightly different times, and the receiver must wait for all lines before the next set of bits can be sent.

3. What is the difference between half-duplex and full-duplex?

Half-duplex: data can travel in both directions along a single connection, but not at the same time (e.g., walkie talkies). Full-duplex: data can travel in both directions simultaneously using two cables or fibres (e.g., Ethernet, fibre optic).

4. Give one benefit and one drawback of USB.

Benefit: Hot-swappable (can be plugged in/removed whilst the device is on); simple connector without screws; supports many devices via drivers.
Drawback: Limited to ~5 metres before a hub is needed; not fast enough for internal devices like CPU and RAM (DDR4 is ~10× faster than USB 3).
